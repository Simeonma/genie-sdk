Sources and monitors

The source injects a known field; monitors record what the structure does to it. Everything measurable in a simulation is decided by how these two are paired. This page gives the time/frequency math and the practical pairing rules.

A simulation result is only as meaningful as its excitation and its measurement chain. The source determines which frequencies exist in the problem at all: a narrow pulse interrogates a wide band coarsely, a long narrowband run interrogates one frequency precisely, and no amount of post-processing can recover spectral detail the source never injected. The monitors determine what is recorded of that response, and their placement and sampling fix the ceiling on what the recorded data can tell. Getting the pairing wrong produces simulations that run cleanly and finish quickly and answer a question that was not asked.

#The source waveform

All sources share one temporal envelope — a Gaussian-modulated cosine:

J(t) = Re{ E₀ · e − (t − t₀)²/(2σ²) · e − i · 2π · f₀ · t }

Its Fourier transform is again a Gaussian centered at f₀:

|J̃(f)| ∝ exp(−(f − f₀)² · 2π²σ² )

With σ in seconds, the transform is exact and analytic:

J̃(f) = √(2π) · σ · E₀ · exp(−2π²σ²(f − f₀)² )

The Gaussian is the standard choice for a reason beyond convenience. It is the unique pulse whose spectral width is minimum for its temporal width, so it wastes the least simulation time per unit of spectral coverage. It also has no sidelobes: unlike a rectangular or sinc-shaped envelope, its spectrum decays monotonically, so a pulse meant for one band does not sneak energy into distant resonances of the structure.

The SDK parameters map as: freq = f₀ (Hz), bandwidth = the Gaussian bandwidth parameter (Hz). One convention to know: bandwidth is not itself the spectral FWHM (full width at half maximum) — it is the historical rate parameter, pinned by the kernel as σ_t = 0.5/bw, giving effective widths of time-amplitude FWHM ≈ 1.18/bw, time-intensity FWHM ≈ 0.83/bw, and spectral-intensity FWHM ≈ 0.53·bw. A pulse with bandwidth bw resolves spectral features coarser than roughly bw — and, by time-bandwidth duality, its duration is ~1/bw, so wide bandwidth = short pulse = short simulation. delay shifts t₀ (useful to let the pulse fully enter before hitting the structure), phase adds a constant phase.

Worked numbers.For f₀ = 300 THz and bw = 120 THz: with σ = 0.5/bw, the pulse duration is ΔtFWHM ≈ 1.18/(120×10¹²) ≈ 9.8 fs. That is about three optical cycles at 300 THz (one cycle is 1/f₀ ≈ 3.3 fs), so the pulse is close to the extreme of what "narrowband carrier plus envelope" language still describes, and its spectral-intensity FWHM is ≈ 64 THz centered on f₀ (meaningful content spans roughly f₀ ± bw/2). This is the right source for broadband transmission through a photonic-crystal slab; it is the wrong source for a narrow filter, where 100 THz of spectrum is spent on frequencies that are never inspected while the filter line itself receives only a few GHz of honest resolution.

At the other extreme, consider a cavity measurement run for T = 100 fs. The achievable spectral resolution is Δf = 1/T = 10 THz. A cavity with Q = 10000 at 300 THz has a linewidth f₀/Q = 30 MHz, five orders of magnitude finer than the 10 THz grid of frequencies the run can distinguish. The cavity's response exists in the data only as an unresolved spike on a coarse comb; its Q cannot be reported, and even its center frequency is uncertain to the grid spacing. High-Q measurements are budgeted in total run time, not in source bandwidth, which is the central lesson of this page.

#Choosing a source type

TypeField injectedUse whenDon't use when
add_plane_waveuniform on a plane (TFSF formalism)far-field excitation, scattering problemsthe beam-spot shape matters (a plane wave has no spot)
add_gaussian_beamplane wave × transverse Gaussian envelopefocused / finite beamsthe aperture radius exceeds the domain's transverse extent (the beam is truncated at the boundary)
add_soft_sourcelocal current volume J(r)waveguide feeds, point dipoles, custom injectionmeasuring transmission directly — a soft source radiates in both directions

The choice follows from how the real experiment illuminates the structure. A solar-cell or metasurface calculation lives in the plane wave limit because the illumination is kilometers away relative to the device; a lens-coupled sensor needs the Gaussian beam because the focusing geometry is part of the physics; a waveguide ring resonator is fed through its port waveguide, and only a soft source inside that waveguide reproduces the mode-matched injection without modeling an actual laser.

The plane wave is a total-field/scattered-field source: fields inside its window follow a calibrated incident waveform; the structure scatters against it. Place it on a face with from_face('z_min'), or steer with theta/phi — for oblique incidence with periodic boundaries, the boundary must be bloch (see 03 · Boundaries).

The TFSF machinery matters because it keeps the incident field defined even where a pure field-injection scheme would fail: the source surface imposes the known incoming wave, and everywhere else the solver evolves total fields, so the scattered field is obtained by subtraction against the analytic incident wave rather than by a second reference run. The practical consequence is that a plane wave simulation needs the source plane and the monitors to respect the TFSF geometry: monitors between source and structure measure incident plus scattered fields and are not transmission by themselves.

The one-way behavior is measurable, and the two source families sit at opposite ends of it. A soft source observed at equal distances in front of and behind its plane shows peak amplitudes differing by 0.00% — exactly bidirectional. A plane wave measured the same way shows |Ey| = 0.479 ahead of the source versus 0.0383 behind it, a ~12× contrast; the back-side residue is numerical leakage from the TFSF surface, not a second beam.

The Gaussian beam's radius is the aperture radius on the source plane: the 1/e² intensity beam width is w ≈ 0.47 · radius.

This distinction is a recurring source of factor-of-two confusion. If an experiment quotes a beam waist w₀ = 2 μm, radius must be set to ≈ 4.3 μm, not 2 μm. Feeding the waist value directly produces a beam twice as broad as intended, and the mismatch is invisible in field plots: the beam still looks like a beam. Verify the actual profile once with a transverse field slice at the source plane, or by comparing the numerically propagated divergence against the Gaussian prediction θ ≈ λ/(πw).

#Frequency monitors are DFTs

A freq monitor does not store time series. As the wave passes, it accumulates, per requested frequency fₖ:

Ẽ(fₖ) = Σₙ  E(tₙ) · w(tₙ) · e + i · 2π · fₖ · tₙ · Δt

a discrete Fourier transform at exactly the n points requested via .freq(center, bw, n) — they linspace across [center − bw/2, center + bw/2]. Spectral resolution is limited by the simulated time: Δf ≈ 1/totaltime, so a Q = 1000 resonance at 300 THz needs ~5 ps of total time to resolve. apodize applies the window w(t) to suppress truncation artifacts; down_sample thins storage, not physics.

The accumulated-complex-sum formulation is what makes broadband FDTD spectroscopy affordable: the time series is never stored, and each requested frequency costs one complex multiply-add per time step per field component. The corollary is that the frequency list is frozen when the simulation starts. The band should be requested generously (a few tens of points across the feature of interest is usually enough, since Lorentzian lines are smooth between samples), because there is no re-sampling later from the same run.

Resolution and leakage are quantifiable. A finite record of length T is a rectangle window; its transform is a sinc with first sidelobe at −13.2 dB and first zero at 1/T — that sinc convolves the spectrum and rings around sharp features. The Hann window (apodize) trades 2× main-lobe width for −31.5 dB sidelobes:

Δf = 1/T ,   Wrect(f) = T · sinc(fT) ,   Whann: sidelobe ≤ 10 − 31.5/10

Rule: resolve two peaks ⇒ Δf < |f₁ − f₂|; kill ringing around a sharp resonance ⇒ apodize('both') and accept the broadened lineshape.

Trusted-band rule: the trustworthy content of a Gaussian-pulse run is f₀ ± bandwidth/2; outside that interval the spectrum shows only the pulse's roll-off, not physics.

Apodization is worth its cost: measured on a reference run, it brings the in-band spectral-shape deviation from the percent level down to below the percent level. A width set too narrow, however, throws the window's own sidebands out of band and forfeits the benefit.

The −13.2 dB number is worth memorizing because it sets the dynamic range of any unwindowed spectrum. Sidelobes only 18 dB below the main peak mean that a weak feature sitting within a few 1/T of a strong one is buried under the strong feature's ringing, and it will look like physics — ripples have structure and invite over-interpretation. When a spectrum shows a comb of ripples with spacing 1/T around a sharp line, that comb is the rectangle window, not resonances; a rerun with apodize is warranted before it is treated as physics.

Time monitors (add_time) store the raw series E(tₙ) — use them to see pulses, reflections, and to check that the field has actually decayed before the run ends.

The decay check deserves emphasis because it is the single cheapest error detector in the workflow. A time monitor placed where the residual field is visible shows at a glance whether the run ended while the structure was still ringing; a spectrum computed from a truncated ring-down is a convolution of the true lineshape with a sinc, which broadens the line, moves its apparent center, and decorates it with sidelobes. Tutorial 6 turns this into a full checklist.

#Normalization, end to end

Transmission and reflection are ratios, and the ratio is what removes the source from the measurement. The canonical power measurement is a face monitor behind the structure, with the same size as the incident beam, integrating the normal Poynting flux:

P(f) = 1⁄2 ∫ Re{ E × H* } · n̂ dA        T(f) = P(f) / Pinc(f)

The complete procedure uses two runs over the identical grid, sources, and monitor set: one with the structure, one with the structure removed (replaced by the background medium). The structure-less run's face monitor records the incident flux P_inc(f), and the full run's monitor records P(f); the physical transmission is the ratio T(f) = P(f)/P_inc(f).

The division cancels more than the source spectrum. It also cancels the Gaussian window in the DFT kernel, the grid discretization error common to both runs, and any constant phase or amplitude offset of the source, because every multiplicative factor appearing in both numerator and denominator drops out. What survives is a property of the structure alone, which is why a normalized spectrum is comparable across runs, across grids, and against experiment, while raw |Ẽ|² from a single run is comparable to nothing.

For reflection, with a TFSF plane wave, incident and scattered fields superpose linearly; the reflected amplitude is the scattered field on a second face monitor placed between the source and the structure. The incident reference can be obtained from the structure-less run's same face, since with nothing to reflect, its field there is the incident wave. With R and T in hand, R + T + A = 1 becomes the conservation check — if it fails, either the box is too small (light bypassing a monitor) or the run truncated a resonance. Reflection from oblique or anisotropic problems may need the two polarization components separated before the flux ratio is taken.

In practice much of this is automated: the server computes reference fluxes and reports normalized transmission in digest() (the throughput numbers marked normalized=true are physical T; face monitors only — point monitors have no area integral and report no T). Automation is not a substitute for understanding, though, because it is exactly the runs where the reference was invalid (wrong monitor size, structure spilling out of the reference aperture) that automation faithfully propagates into a wrong but plausible-looking number.

"The same size as the incident beam" is doing real work in that sentence. A monitor smaller than the beam clips power and reads a low transmission; a monitor larger than the beam is safe in principle but collects stray power that bypassed the structure through the boundaries, which reads high. The conservative choice is a monitor spanning the full cross-section of the domain for collimated problems, and a beam-sized monitor only once the scattered field outside the beam aperture has been verified negligible.

The conservation check deserves promotion to habit. Absorption computed as 1 − R − T is only as good as the flux collection, and a failing check is information about the simulation, not about the material: light escaping through the lateral boundaries, a monitor clipping a diffracted order, or a resonance still ringing at cutoff each produce a characteristic deficit. A few percent of unexplained missing power is a bug to find, not a rounding error to wave past.
