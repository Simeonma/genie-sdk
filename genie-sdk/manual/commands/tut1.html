What is FDTD?

FDTD (Finite-Difference Time-Domain) solves Maxwell's equations directly on a space grid, stepping in time. This page gives the minimal math needed to understand every knob in the SDK. Only vector calculus is assumed.

FDTD occupies a specific niche among computational electromagnetics methods. Frequency-domain methods (finite elements, method of moments) solve one frequency at a time and pay for a matrix solve; FDTD instead integrates the fields forward in time with no matrix at all. A broadband answer falls out of a single run, because a short pulse injected into the domain carries all frequencies of interest and every monitor records their superposition. The price is explicit: the time step is capped by the grid spacing (the CFL (Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy) stability limit; see below), so a run that resolves finer structure needs proportionally more steps. Every parameter in this SDK is a lever on one of three things: how space is gridded, how time is stepped, and how materials enter the update.

#The equations being solved

Maxwell's curl equations in full generality: ε and μ are in general tensor distributions of position, ε = ε̄(r), μ = μ̄(r) (anisotropy included; isotropic media are the scalar special case) — in SI units, lossless (no conductivity term), with electric current J and magnetic current M:

∂H/∂t = −μ−1(r) ∇×E − μ−1(r) M
∂E/∂t = ε−1(r) ∇×H − ε−1(r) J

Everything a FDTD code does is: discretize space, discretize time, and integrate these two equations alternately. Given E at time n, the update yields H at n+½; from H at n+½ it yields E at n+1. This is the leapfrog scheme — explicit, second-order accurate, and it needs no matrix inversion, which is why the method maps well to GPUs.

The reason this alternation is legitimate rather than an approximation heaped on an approximation is the structure of the equations themselves: ∂H/∂t depends only on E and ∂E/∂t only on H. The two curl equations form a coupled pair of first-order ODEs in time at every point of space, and leapfrog integrates such pairs with the derivative evaluated at the offset time, which is exactly centered in the interval it spans. The result is second-order accurate in Δt with no extra work. The two source terms live in different equations: J appears only in the E update — this is where the SDK injects f.src objects, and it is the only place the exterior world ever touches the simulation — while M appears only in the H update. The solver keeps M in the equations for generality but does not expose it: everything f.src produces is an electric current density.

#The Yee grid

FDTD does not put all field components at the same points. The Yee staggered grid offsets E and H by half a cell in each direction:

Ex  at (i + 1⁄2, j,   k)      Hx  at (i,   j + 1⁄2, k + 1⁄2)
Ey  at (i,   j + 1⁄2, k)      Hy  at (i + 1⁄2, j,   k + 1⁄2)
Ez  at (i,   j,   k + 1⁄2)    Hz  at (i + 1⁄2, j + 1⁄2, k)

This staggering makes ∇×E centered-difference accurate to second order and automatically satisfies charge conservation. It is also why a "cell" in the output can be half a step away from where a source is nominally placed — the grid, not the geometry, defines the sample points.

The staggering is not an implementation convenience; it is the discrete image of the integral form of Ampère's law. Each E component sits on a face of the cell and each H component on an edge piercing that face, so the loop of four E samples around an H sample is exactly a discrete line integral of E around the edge, and the difference of four H samples around an E sample is exactly a discrete circulation of H on the face. Both differences are centered, which is where the second-order accuracy comes from, and the arrangement enforces a discrete ∇·B = 0 to machine precision for as long as the simulation runs. The practical consequence is positional: when the SDK reports that a monitor is at x = 1.7 μm, it is placed at the nearest sampling location of the requested component, which may be up to half a cell from that coordinate. With a 15 nm grid the discrepancy is below any physically meaningful accuracy, but it explains small systematic offsets when monitors on different components are compared.

#The discrete update (3D, lossless)

The Yee staggering turns the curls into centered differences. Writing E|_{i,j,k}^n for E_x(i+½,j,k) at time nΔt, the z-component update is (shown for an isotropic medium — ε is the rasterized scalar sampled at that E location; anisotropic media run the same stencil with the tensor inverse ε−1 component-wise. Sources are dropped in this lossless, source-free form and re-enter exactly as the J term of the continuous equation):

Ez|n + 1 = Ez|^n + (Δt/ε) · [ (Hy|n + 1⁄2 − Hy|n + 1⁄2i − 1) / Δx
 −(Hx|n + 1⁄2 − Hx|n + 1⁄2j − 1) / Δy ]

and the magnetic update mirrors it with roles swapped and a half-step offset:

Hx|n + 1⁄2 = Hx|n − 1⁄2 − (Δt/μ) · [ (Ez|j − Ez|j + 1) / Δy
 +(Ey|k + 1 − Ey|k) / Δz ]

This has two immediate consequences.

1. Second order everywhere. Centered differences and leapfrog are both O(Δx², Δt²) — halving dx buys 4× field accuracy at 8× cost (2× cells per axis × 2× steps). 2. Materials enter as a local coefficient. The rasterized material arrays from f.mat/f.geom are looked up per component — scalar 1/ε(r) for isotropic media, the tensor inverse for anisotropic ones; that is the whole material interface.

A close look at the update shows that each new value depends only on its own four neighbors and its own past value. There is no global coupling anywhere in the scheme, which is why the kernel is a stencil sweep over a regular array: perfectly coalesced memory access, no synchronization, near peak GPU throughput. It also means a discontinuity in ε is handled without any special code; the field at the material interface simply updates with a different coefficient on each side. The physics of reflection and refraction at that interface emerges from the update; it is not put in by hand. The cost of this locality is that all knowledge the solver has about the structure is the ε(r) array, so whatever errors the rasterization introduced (staircasing of curved boundaries, half-cell placement of facets) are errors the solver will faithfully propagate.

#What the solver does per step, in code terms

Stripped of all infrastructure, one time step is the following loop, with every array a regular 3D grid of float32 on the GPU:

for n in range(n_steps):
    # H update: half a step behind E in time, half a cell offset in space
    H += -(dt/mu) * (curl(E) + M)        # stencil over 6 neighbor reads
    apply_H_boundary(H)                  # PML/periodic correction terms
    if src_active(n): J = src_profile(n) # inject source into E next
    E += (dt/eps) * (curl(H) - J)        # stencil, symmetric to H update
    apply_E_boundary(E)
    for m in monitors: m.accumulate(E, H, n)   # running DFT (discrete Fourier transform) or time sample

Three things in this loop explain otherwise mysterious behavior. First, the source is a term in the E update, not a separate object living in the grid: a plane wave is implemented as an equivalent surface current on the injection face, which is why a source can sit on a PML face and still radiate one way. Second, boundaries are applied as corrections after each update, so a PML is a set of extra auxiliary variables updated alongside E and H; the cost of layers = 12 is a modest fraction of the total grid. Third, monitor accumulation happens every step inside the loop at effectively zero marginal cost, which is why a 300-point DFT monitor is free compared to the march itself, and so there is no reluctance to add monitors: unused data is cheap, a rerun is not.

#The discrete dispersion relation

In continuous vacuum the dispersion relation is ω = c · k. On the Yee grid (3D) the discrete relation is

(1/(cΔt)) · sin(ωΔt/2) = 
  √( sin²(kxΔx/2)/Δx² + sin²(kyΔy/2)/Δy² + sin²(kzΔz/2)/Δz² )

which reduces to ω = c · k only as Δx,Δt → 0. Expanding at small k·dx gives a leading phase error of order (k · dx)²/24 per wavelength: waves slow down and, off-axis, slow down anisotropically. Keeping dx ≤ λ/(10 · n) bounds the phase error to ≤0.1% per wavelength on-axis, and refinement is warranted if the observable depends on interference between paths of very different lengths.

The anisotropy deserves attention because it is the error FDTD users actually get bitten by. On the grid, a wave traveling along a grid axis propagates more slowly than one traveling along the diagonal, with a velocity difference of order a fraction of a percent at ten cells per wavelength. A single propagating pulse barely notices. An interferometric observable, where a weak signal is the difference between two paths, will register the differential phase error directly. The worked rule: at dx = λ/(10 · n) the on-axis phase lag per wavelength is (2π/10)²/24 ≈ 1.6×10⁻² rad before the Δt term partially cancels it; empirically the Yee scheme's time-stepping error offsets the spatial error on-axis, leaving combined errors near 10⁻³–10⁻⁴ per wavelength. Concrete case: a 25 nm grid in silicon (n ≈ 3.5) at free-space λ₀ = 1550 nm resolves the material wavelength λ/n = 443 nm with about 17.7 cells, safely inside the rule. The same 25 nm grid in silica (n ≈ 1.45) gives 30 cells per wavelength, which is finer than needed; that headroom is better spent refining the silicon region only, which is precisely what f.mesh.refine is for.

#The CFL time-step limit

Explicit time stepping is only stable if a signal cannot cross a cell in one step. In 3D with spacings dx, dy, dz:

Δt ≤ 1 / ( c · √(1/dx² + 1/dy² + 1/dz²) )

The SDK exposes this as f.domain.cfl (default 0.9): the actual Δt is cfl × the stability limit. It normally needs no adjustment.

The physical reading of the inequality: the fastest signal the discrete scheme can carry is c, and in one step Δt the update stencil couples each component to neighbors at distances dx, dy, dz. If cΔt exceeds 1/√(1/dx² + 1/dy² + 1/dz²), information traverses the stencil faster than the finite difference can represent, and round-off grows without bound; the run diverges to NaN within a few hundred steps. The factor 0.9 is a safety margin, not a tuning knob: it exists because material interfaces and PML regions locally stiffen the discrete problem. Lowering cfl further costs steps without buying accuracy, since the scheme's accuracy is dominated by Δx, not Δt, once stability holds.

Worked number. For the common cubic case dx = dy = dz = 25 nm the stability limit is Δtmax = 25×10⁻⁹ / (c√3) = 4.81×10⁻¹⁷ s ≈ 0.0481 fs, and with the default cfl = 0.9 the solver runs at Δt ≈ 0.0433 fs. A run with total_time = 100 fs then costs nsteps ≈ 100 / 0.0433 ≈ 2310 steps. The same formulas on the anisotropic Quickstart grid (dx = 15 nm, dy = dz = 30 nm) give 1/√(1/dx² + 1/dy² + 1/dz²) = 1/(8.17×10⁷ m⁻¹) = 4.08×10⁻¹⁷ s, so Δt ≈ 0.0367 fs: refining one axis by 2× raised the step count per simulated femtosecond from about 23 to about 27. The step count is set by the finest axis, which is the honest way to price local refinement.

The total simulated time is f.domain.total_time (seconds) — the single time knob. At build the server derives the step count:

nsteps ≈ totaltime / Δt

n_steps is a read-only derived value (reported in summary/results); f.run() takes no step argument — the job simply marches until the grid's time capacity (total_time) is reached.

#Numerical dispersion

On a grid, waves of wavelength λ propagate at slightly the wrong speed: the phase error scales roughly as (2π · dx/λ)². The standard rule of thumb is at least 10 cells per wavelength in the highest-index material (dx ≈ λ/(10 · n)), and finer near resonant structures. That is exactly what f.mesh.dx and f.mesh.refine(..., factor=k) control:

f.mesh.dx = 25e-9                    # nominal spacing everywhere
f.mesh.refine((−0.5e−6, 0.5e−6),     # …with 2× refinement in a box
              (−0.5e−6, 0.5e−6),
              (0, 0.3e−6), factor=2)

Resonators deserve the finer language. A cavity of quality factor Q stores the field for Q/(π f₀) of time, i.e. Q optical cycles divided by π, and over that lifetime the numerical phase error accumulated at Δv/v ≈ 10⁻⁴ shifts the resonance by roughly f₀·10⁻⁴. For a resonance at 200 THz that is a 20 GHz shift, which is easily visible in a high-resolution spectrum. The rule of thumb "finer near resonant structures" is therefore not about resolving geometry: it is about keeping the total phase error accumulated over the ring-down below the shift intended to be measured. If the observable is a broadband transmission spectrum with 30 GHz features, 10–15 cells per wavelength suffices; if it is a Q = 10⁴ mode frequency to within 0.1%, refinement should be budgeted accordingly and verified by halving dx once.

#What a simulation actually computes

1. Build: the config (materials, geometry) is rasterized onto the Yee grid → arrays ε(r), μ(r). 2. March: source injects J; leapfrog updates E/H for n_steps; monitors accumulate their data as the wave passes. 3. Read out: monitors return either raw time series (add_time) or DFTs at chosen frequencies (add_freq).

The build stage is a one-time cost, typically seconds, and it is where geometry mistakes surface as unexpected ε(r) — a frequency monitor placed in the wrong material produces plausible-looking but wrong spectra without any warning, so it is worth a f.digest() check before the march. The march dominates the runtime and scales as ncells × nsteps, both of which are under direct control. The read-out is server-side and free. The overall economics are worth internalizing early: for a fixed grid, doubling total_time doubles cost but adds spectral resolution δf ≈ 1/totaltime and ring-down fidelity; halving dx costs roughly 16× but quadruples field accuracy. Deciding which resource the observable actually needs is most of the craft in setting up FDTD simulations.
