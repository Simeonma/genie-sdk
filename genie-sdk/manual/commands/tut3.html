Boundaries and domain size

A simulation box must end somewhere. What happens at the end decides whether the results are physics or reflections. This page gives the math behind the five boundary kinds and the rules for sizing the box.

Boundary choice and domain sizing are where most first FDTD simulations go wrong, and they go wrong quietly. A reflecting boundary does not crash the run; it superimposes a delayed, aliased copy of the field onto every monitor, producing spectra with ripples that look like physics (Fabry–Perot fringes of the computational box) and are in fact artifacts. The tell is that the fringes move when the boundary moves and stay when the structure moves. The discipline on this page is: choose each face's condition deliberately, size the box against the wave, and size the time against the slowest transient the observable depends on.

#Why the boundary matters

The grid is finite, but the wave is not: without treatment, a wave reaching ∂Ω reflects back with near-unity amplitude and contaminates every monitor. The boundary condition is a model of the exterior world, and it is chosen per face:

f.domain.boundary_condition('z_min', 'pml')
f.domain.boundary_condition('x_min', 'periodic')

The per-face granularity is a feature, not a bookkeeping burden. A device that is translationally invariant along one axis needs periodic (or bloch) on that pair of faces and absorption elsewhere; a waveguide simulation wants PML on the propagation faces and metal or periodic on the sides. A symmetric structure under symmetric excitation admits a symmetry plane: replacing half the box with a pmc (or pec, for the odd counterpart) symmetry plane and simulating the half domain halves the computation — an explicit selection rule, not a convenience: whenever the structure and the excitation share a mirror symmetry, pmc/pec on the symmetry plane is the correct boundary and the half-domain simulation is the cheaper equivalent of the full one. pec and pmc are the only kinds with no same-axis pairing requirement; a single symmetry face is a valid configuration. Reading the boundary list is therefore reading a compact statement of the symmetry and radiation geometry of the problem. If the exterior world on each of the six faces cannot be stated in one sentence, the simulation is not yet specified.

#PML — the absorbing boundary

The Perfectly Matched Layer truncates the grid with an artificial layer that absorbs incoming waves with (in the ideal continuous case) zero reflection at any angle and frequency. It works by coordinate stretching: inside the layer, the derivative normal to the boundary is warped as

∂/∂x  →  ∂/∂x · 1/s(x),     s(x) = κ(x) + i · σ(x)/ω

The imaginary part σ makes the layer lossy with no impedance mismatch at the interface; fields entering it decay as exp(−σ · x/(c · εeff)) and never come back. Discretization makes the match slightly imperfect; reflection is exponentially small in the layer parameters:

|R| ∼ exp(−2 · σmax · L / (c · neff))
Why it matches.In an ordinary lossy layer the wave impedance Z = √(μ/(ε + iσ/ω)) depends on σ — the impedance jumps at the interface and reflects. In PML the coordinate stretching s multiplies both ε and μ identically, so the ratio is untouched:
Zpml = √(μ · s / (ε · s)) = √(μ/ε) = Zmedium

Perfect matching is a property of the continuous stretching; the discrete layer inherits it only approximately, and the residual reflection of a ramped layer integrates to

R(θ) ∝ exp(−2 · (cosθ/c) · ∫₀ᴸ σ(x′) dx′ )

which is the precise statement of "exponentially small": digits of reflection are bought with layer thickness or σ peak, exponentially. A polynomial ramp σ(x) = σmax · (x/L)^order minimizes discretization mismatch at both ends — that is the SDK's order parameter (default 3).

The ramp matters because the dominant residual reflection of a discrete PML comes from the leading edge, where the ramp is shallow but the staircase sampling of σ is coarsest relative to the wavelength. A higher order holds σ near zero longer, easing the field into the loss, at the price of a steeper kick near the outer edge. Order 3 is the standard compromise; order 4 occasionally helps for grazing incidence, where the effective path length through the layer stretches by 1/cosθ and the reflection constant degrades long before the wave reaches the outer edge.

Worked number. For an order = 3 ramp the integral of σ over the layer is σmax · L/4 (the average of (x/L)³ is ¼), so at normal incidence the reflection amplitude is R ≈ exp(−σmax · L/(2c)). To hit −40 dB (|R| ≤ 10⁻²) requires σmax · L ≥ 2c · ln(100) = 2.76×10⁹. With the Quickstart grid (dx = 15 nm) and the default layers = 12, the layer is L = 180 nm thick, which requires σmax ≥ 1.5×10¹⁰ s⁻¹. That is a modest demand and the default meets it; the case that does not is a beam arriving at 85° incidence, where cosθ = 0.09 inflates the required σmax · L by a factor of 11 and the honest fix is more layers, not a bigger σ peak (which aggravates the discretization mismatch the ramp was chosen to suppress). For evanescent tails — a resonator's near field decaying into the boundary — κ stretching (kappa_max) does the work, since an evanescent wave decays in the layer only through the real stretch.

The three knobs in the SDK map directly: layers = layer thickness L, kappa_max = peak real stretch κ, order = polynomial order of the σ ramp; defaults and the layers ≥ 2 validity floor are tabulated in 01 · f.domain. Layer-count practice: 12–16 layers for accuracy runs, 8 layers for quick iterations; increase layers for grazing-incidence or highly evanescent fields.

#Periodic and Bloch boundaries

periodic ties opposite faces: the field satisfies

E(r + a · x̂) = E(r)

valid only for normal incidence on a repeating structure. For oblique incidence the phase advance between unit cells must be represented — that is the bloch condition:

E(r + a · x̂) = E(r) · ei · kx · a

with k = (kx, ky, kz) in rad/m, or from_source=True to take k from the plane wave already placed. For a plane wave of vacuum wavelength λ₀ at incidence angle θ in a background index n:

|k| = 2π · n/λ₀ ,    kx = |k| · sinθ ,    a · kx = 2π · (a/λ) · sinθ
The `k` protocol.The Bloch wave vector is passed as the full vector k = [kx, ky, kz] in rad/m, the same vector on both faces of the axis, and each face uses only its own axis component. k = [0, 0, 0] makes bloch degenerate to periodic face by face — a usable sanity check that a bloch setup reproduces the periodic result at zero phase advance.

The period enters only as the phase advance per cell — a Bloch sweep is a scan of a·k_x, which is why using periodic where Bloch is needed silently computes the wrong interference — this is the single most common boundary mistake in periodic metasurface work.

The failure mode deserves its emphasis because nothing breaks. With periodic faces and an oblique plane-wave source, the source injects a wave with the correct angle and the boundary immediately contradicts it: the field arriving at x_max with phase advance e^{i·k_x·a} is wrapped to x_min with phase 1, and the mismatch acts as a fictitious scatterer at the boundary. The run completes, the monitors report smooth spectra, and the answer is the interference pattern of the device plus a spurious grating of period 2π/Δkx. from_source=True removes the arithmetic: the solver reads the in-plane wavevector off the source definition. One worked value: the Quickstart source at f = 330 THz (λ₀ = 0.91 μm) striking a grating of period a = 180 nm at θ = 30° in vacuum carries a · kx = 2π · (0.18/0.91) · 0.5 = 0.62 rad of phase advance per cell; that number, not the angle, is the physical input, and it is what a Bloch sweep of angle actually varies.

from_source precedence. When from_source=True is passed together with an explicit k, from_source takes precedence and the explicit k is silently ignored. from_source=True requires a plane-wave or Gaussian-beam source to exist; otherwise the job fails.

Oblique incidence, complete recipe. Fix the angle θ on the source; set transverse bloch faces with k equal to the transverse wave vector at the center frequency (kx = |k(f₀)| · sinθ, with |k| = n · 2πf₀/c); set pml on the propagation (longitudinal) faces. The Bloch phase is exact only at f₀; over a broadband run the off-center frequencies carry the wrong phase advance and accuracy degrades as the bandwidth widens. The shortcut is from_source=True, which reads the transverse wave vector off the source definition.

#Sizing the box

Sizing must satisfy two independent requirements:

Room for the structure.Between any scatterer and a PML face leave several cells of background (and never put a monitor inside the PML). Fields decay there; monitors inside the layer measure garbage.
Runout for the physics.The total time must be long enough that the observable (a pulse, a resonance ring-down) has happened. A resonance with quality factor Q at frequency f₀ decays as
E(t) ∝ e − π · f₀ · t/Q

so roughly totaltime ≳ Q/(π · f₀) is needed to capture the ring-down — too short a time truncates it and produces spectral artifacts (that is what apodize on freq monitors mitigates, not cures).

The two requirements fail in opposite directions and are cheap to check separately. Spatial runout fails as reflections: the rule of thumb is a background gap of order λ/4 to λ/2 between the last scatterer and a PML face, enough that the near field (which decays fast) has become a propagating field (which the PML absorbs at specification) before the truncation. Temporal runout fails as spectral leakage: truncating a ring-down in time convolves the true spectrum with a sinc of width 1/T, so a resonance that still has amplitude at t = T appears broadened and shifted. The apodization window trades that sinc for a Gaussian blur; it is first aid, not a substitute for a long enough T.

domain.total_time (seconds, > 0) is the single definition of the time budget — the derived step count and the absence of a settable n_steps are documented in 01 · f.domain.

#A worked domain-sizing decision

Here is the full sizing computation for the Quickstart grating, as it would be redone for an arbitrary device. The structure spans x ∈ [−1.6, 0.02] μm, so a box of x ∈ [−2, 2] μm leaves a 0.4 μm gap between the last tooth and the x_max PML — about 0.44 vacuum wavelengths at the 330 THz center frequency (λ₀ = 0.91 μm), comfortably above the λ/4 rule. The transverse extent of ±1 μm against a grating that is 1 μm wide leaves 0.5 μm of background on each side; with PML on x_min/x_max only and the default (periodic or metal, per the config) on the transverse faces, the monitor at x = 1.7 μm with a 1.2 μm aperture captures the beam without clipping.

The grid at dx = 15 nm gives 4 μm/15 nm ≈ 267 cells along x and 2 μm/30 nm ≈ 67 along y and z, about 1.2×10⁶ cells total, of which the twelve PML layers per x-face (180 nm, λ₀/5) are a modest overhead. The CFL limit follows the code canon Δt = cfl · mincell/(√3 · c) (on a uniform grid this is equivalent to 1/(c · √(1/dx² + 1/dy² + 1/dz²))). With min_cell = 15 nm and cfl = 0.9 the solver steps at Δt ≈ 2.6×10⁻¹⁷ s. The requested total_time = 200 fs therefore corresponds to 200 fs/0.026 fs ≈ 7700 steps, and since the job marches to the grid's time capacity, all 200 fs of simulated time are delivered. Is that enough? The light must cross the 3.7 μm from source face to monitor, about 12.5 fs, and the grating is a weak, non-resonant scatterer with an effective Q of order 10, whose stored energy decays in Q/(π f₀) ≈ 10 fs. A budget of 200 fs covers a dozen transit times plus full decay, with margin for the low-frequency tail of the 60 THz pulse. Had this been a high-Q resonator instead — say Q = 1000 at f₀ = 193 THz, needing Q/(π f₀) ≈ 1.65 ps — the same arithmetic says the run needs ≈ 63 000 steps, and stopping at 200 fs would smear every spectral feature by more than 1/T ≈ 5 GHz — set a larger total_time instead. One repeatable lesson: the time budget is decided by λ/(n · dx) and Q/(π f₀) together, and both are known before the first run.
