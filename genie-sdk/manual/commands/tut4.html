Materials — dispersion models

FDTD needs the material response ε(ω) in a form that can be evaluated in the time domain. The kernel accepts two families: a constant ε, and a sum of Lorentz/Drude resonance poles. This page gives both models, their parameters, and when each is appropriate.

FDTD solves Maxwell's equations as local difference equations on a grid, stepping forward in time, so every quantity in the update must be computable at the current time step from values the solver already holds. A tabulated ε(ω) is the wrong currency: converting it to the time domain means a convolution with a susceptibility stretching over the whole simulation history, exactly the cost a time-domain method cannot afford. The way out is to restrict materials to response functions that obey a small number of local differential equations in time; both families on this page have that property, and it alone is the real admission criterion. Physical accuracy is a separate question, handled at the end of this page.

#Where ε(ω) comes from

The response is defined by the polarization P(t) to a field E(t), with D = ε₀E + P. In the linear, time invariant case the most general local response is a convolution — frequency domain multiplication:

P(t) = ε₀ ∫ χ(t − t′) E(t′) dt′   ⇔   D̃(ω) = ε₀ (1 + χ̃(ω)) Ẽ(ω) = ε₀ ε(ω) Ẽ(ω)

Causality (χ(t) = 0 for t < 0) forces ε(ω) to be analytic with Re/Im related by Kramers–Kronig — a constant ε is the trivial χ̃ = const case, and every resonant model must reproduce the KK fall-off χ ∼ 1/ω² at high frequency. That requirement is exactly what the pole form satisfies naturally.

Causality is not an ornament on the formulas; it is the working tool. It says the real and imaginary parts of ε(ω) cannot be chosen independently, so a fit that gets Re ε right must come with the Im ε that causality demands. When a material model violates this, the solver still runs (the pole ODEs are stable regardless) but the fields are not the response of any causal medium: reflections, delays, and energy bookkeeping are quietly wrong. Pole models built from real oscillator physics satisfy the KK relations by construction.

#Constant materials

The simplest response — polarization follows the field instantaneously:

D(t) = ε₀ · ε · E(t)

Physically this describes the limit in which all bound-charge resonances of the medium sit far above the frequencies being simulated, so within the band the susceptibility looks flat. Glass at visible frequencies is the textbook case: the electronic resonance of silica lies in the deep UV, and across 400–700 nm the refractive index varies by only a few percent. Whenever the band is narrow relative to the nearest resonance, a constant ε is both cheaper and more stable than a fitted dispersion.

f.mat.add_const() takes eps (and optionally mu), each as a scalar (isotropic), a 3-vector [εxx, εyy, εzz] (diagonal anisotropic), or a symmetric 3×3 tensor. Guards: finite, positive main diagonal; symmetric (off-diagonals may be negative — that is optical activity territory). A negative constant ε is rejected: it is nondispersive and numerically unstable; metals must be expressed as poles.

The rejection of negative constant ε deserves explanation, because it occasionally surprises newcomers with a textbook value in hand. A constant negative ε is nondispersive: its magnitude does not decrease at high frequency. Dispersion is what tames the update, since the polarization of a resonant medium cannot follow the field arbitrarily fast and that built-in lag keeps the coupled system stable. Strip the dispersion away and keep only the negative ε, and the solver is asked to support a medium with unlimited instantaneous response strength. Real metals are negative-ε materials together with strong dispersion; the pole representation carries both halves.

#Lorentz poles

A bound-charge resonance — damped harmonic oscillator per unit volume — contributes to the relative permittivity:

ε(ω) = ε∞ + Σⱼ  Δεⱼ · ω₀ⱼ² / (ω₀ⱼ² − ω² − i · γⱼ · ω)

(written in the e − iωt convention used throughout this page).

Each pole is a small physical system: a charge bound to an equilibrium position, driven by the field, losing energy at rate γ. Below the resonance the charge can follow the field and the contribution to ε is positive; above it the charge cannot keep up, the sign of the response reverses, and ε dips. The ε∞ term collects everything faster than the modeled poles: high-energy resonances that matter only as a frequency-independent background offset.

Near ω₀ the pole produces a resonance in Re ε and an absorption peak in Im ε — this single functional form covers dielectric resonances, phonons, and interband transitions of noble metals.

Derivation.A bound charge q of mass m driven by the field obeys
ẍ + γ · ẋ + ω₀² · x = (q/m) · E(t)

Insert the ansatz x = x_ω · e − iωt with E = E_ω · e − iωt, solve for x_ω, and use P = N·q·x with number density N:

χ(ω) = (Nq²/m) / (ω₀² − ω² − iγω) = Δε · ω₀² / (ω₀² − ω² + iγω)

with Δε ≡ Nq²/(mε₀ω₀²) — the three parameters are the oscillator's natural frequency, damping, and static strength. In the time domain this polarization obeys the ODE

dP/dt + γP + ω₀² · ∫P = Δε · ε₀ · ω₀² · E

which FDTD integrates with its own leapfrog, alongside the field update — this is precisely how dispersive materials stay cheap: one extra local ODE per pole, no convolution. The cost scaling is worth internalizing, because it drives pole-count decisions later. Each pole adds one auxiliary field component per grid cell that carries the material, plus the arithmetic of its update, so memory and runtime grow roughly linearly in pole count while a constant material costs nothing extra. The art of material fitting is getting adequate accuracy with the fewest poles the band demands.

Worked numbers.Take a gold-like metal at the green edge of the visible, λ = 550 nm. The angular frequency is ω = 2πc/λ = 2π · (3×10⁸)/(550×10⁻⁹) ≈ 3.4×10¹⁵ rad/s. Handbooks quote Re ε ≈ −5 at this wavelength. That single number says the free electrons are screening strongly: the displacement current through the medium nearly cancels the vacuum displacement current, the field is expelled, and the material behaves optically as a metal rather than a lossy dielectric; a constant-ε model is out of the question and a pole model is mandatory. To reproduce Im ε over a 100 THz window around this frequency the fit typically needs the Drude term plus two to four Lorentz poles: Drude owns the broad screening background and each Lorentz pole covers one interband feature. One pole alone bends ε too slowly to track the steep rise of Im ε near interband thresholds and the slow Drude tail at once, and the fit residuals make this obvious immediately.

#Drude poles

Free carriers (metals, doped semiconductors) are the ω₀ → 0 limit, where the restoring force vanishes:

ε(ω) = ε∞ − ωp² / (ω² + i · γ · ω)

f.mat.add_eps_drude_pole(omega_p, gamma) takes the plasma frequency omega_p > 0 (rad/s) and gamma. A metallic ε with εinf plus one Drude and a few Lorentz poles is the standard model for gold and silver in the visible/NIR.

The physical picture behind the sign is worth carrying around. In a metal the conduction electrons are not bound: pushed by the field, they simply accelerate, accumulating 180° of phase lag within half a cycle for ω below ω_p. The polarization that results opposes the applied field, and ε drops below one, then below zero. Below the plasma edge the wave becomes an evanescent screening field, which is why polished metals reflect; doping a semiconductor moves carriers into the same regime, which is why Drude models also describe plasmonic semiconductors and transparent conducting oxides.

Derivation.Drude is the free-carrier limit of the same oscillator: drop the restoring term (ω₀ → 0) in Newton's equation
dp/dt = −γ · p − q · E(t),   P = N · q · x = N · p/q  ⇒  χ(ω) = −ωp²/(ω² + iγω)

with ωp² = Nq²/(mε₀) the plasma frequency. The sign is the giveaway: Drude contributes negative Re ε below ωp — the screening that makes metals reflective. Note that both derivations share the static strength identity ωp² = Δε · ω₀²; that identity is the kernel's uniform Lorentz storage convention (a lorentz pole stores ωp² = Δε · ω₀²), so each pole is stored once and expanded consistently.

#The sign convention

  • γ < 0 is rejected. Negative damping is not gain — the gain channel is a negative oscillator strength (Δε < 0 on a Lorentz pole, equivalently ωp² < 0), which the kernel accepts as a bounded, stable pump model.
  • ω₀ = 0 is Drude's job. A Lorentz pole with ω₀ = 0 is rejected.

The reasoning behind the first guard is subtle enough to state plainly. Damping with γ > 0 removes energy from the field into heat, which is what passive media do. Flip the sign and the pole pumps energy into every field, everywhere, at all frequencies — an unphysical amplifier with no inversion mechanism, and the simulation diverges. A laser, by contrast, has gain in a specific band with finite saturation, and a negative oscillator strength under a Lorentz lineshape reproduces exactly that: gain concentrated near ω₀, bounded and stable. The kernel keeps the physical door open and closes the divergent one.

#Anisotropy: iso and rank-1 poles

Each pole is either iso (the formulas above apply per axis with the same numbers) or rank-1: supply alpha=[x,y,z] (and optionally beta, default = alpha) to confine the resonance to one direction, giving

ε(ω) = ε∞ · I + Δε · ω₀²/(ω₀² − ω² − iγ · ω) · α̂β̂ᵀ

The rank-1 form describes media whose resonances respond to one field direction but not the orthogonal ones. Carbon nanotube arrays, chain metamaterials, and molecular monolayers all have resonances tied to a structural axis; along it the electrons can move and the pole is strong, across it they cannot and the response is background only. The alternative is to stack two independent sets of axis poles, more flexible but with doubled bookkeeping; for a genuinely single dipolar axis, the rank-1 form is the honest and cheaper encoding.

Direction vectors are normalized automatically; zero vectors are rejected. Axis-independent parameters = stack several poles.

#Getting real material data as poles

Fitting poles by hand is rarely necessary. f.mat.add_from_library('Au', …) loads bundled n-k data, fits ε∞ + poles on the client (identical to the server-side fit), and registers the lorentz node — see 10 · genie.material for fit_range, pole-count trade-offs, and fitting custom measurement data. A bare from_library node in the config is rejected; the error message lists the supported routes.

The workflow exists because handbook data arrives as tables of n and k, never as poles, and the conversion is a nonlinear least-squares problem with real trade-offs: more poles always lower the residual and always raise the simulation cost, and the improvement concentrates at the sharpest spectral feature. The practical strategy is to set fit_range to the band the sources and monitors actually occupy and judge the fit only inside it, because a pole model is a contract for a frequency range, not a global theory of the material.

#Checking a material model before trusting it

Three checks, in increasing order of effort, catch nearly every bad material model before it costs a simulation campaign. First, plot the model: reconstruct ε(ω) from ε∞ and the fitted poles over the band, on a dense frequency grid, and compare Re ε against the tabulated Re ε and Im ε against the tabulated 2nk (since Im ε = 2nk). This plot exposes the two standard failure modes at a glance: a fit that misses Im ε badly around an interband edge means too few poles for the sharpness, and a fit that wiggles between data points means the fitter is interpolating measurement noise rather than physics.

Second, check causality consistency. For a pole model this is automatic in exact arithmetic, so what is really being checked is the plot and conversions: recompute Im ε from the plotted Re ε via the KK relation in outline and confirm they are at least of the same magnitude and shape. A gross mismatch almost always means a transcription error, such as radians confused with Hz (ω₀ = 2πf, so a pole entered in Hz is off by 6.3×) or ε and ε−1 conflated.

Third, compare Im ε against the tabulated nk at a handful of spot frequencies, expressed as absorption. At λ = 550 nm, gold with n ≈ 0.47 and k ≈ 2.4 gives Im ε = 2nk ≈ 2.3; if the fitted model's Im ε there is 0.5, the material transmits far more energy unabsorbed than gold, and every extinction or heating result is wrong in a way no grid refinement repairs. A pole fit is only as trustworthy as this last comparison.
